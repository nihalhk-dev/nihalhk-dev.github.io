The problem

Models decay quietly

Fixed rules are common for deciding when a model needs review, but how many false alarms they give, and how long they take to notice a real drop, is rarely measured.

The method

Next step

Put a price on every alarm

My supervisor's feedback was to close the use case with a model in production and its real intervention costs. The next step is to measure how much money is lost: each false alarm costs a review, and each month a decayed model stays in use costs bad decisions. The threshold would then be chosen to minimise that total cost.

Expected cost

cost = false alarms × review cost
     + months of delay × cost of a decayed month

Both terms come from curves I already measure. What is missing are a bank's real costs.

Field notes

Short write-ups from my work
Model monitoring · 3 min

Why a fixed PSI threshold can hide a model's decay

PSI > 0.25 is a common trigger for reviewing a model. It's simple and useful, but it answers a different question than the one we usually care about.

Read the note

PSI measures how much the population has shifted, not whether the model got worse. The two can move separately: customers can change while the model keeps ranking them well, and a model can lose accuracy on a population that looks stable.

A fixed threshold also ignores sample size. The same 0.25 means something very different with 200 observations than with 20,000. In my synthetic test bench, with about 800 observations per period and drops of the sizes I tested, PSI > 0.25 did not fire in any of 15 scenarios, while detectors calibrated on each model's history did.

That doesn't make PSI useless. It's a good input check. But to know whether performance has really decayed, I'd monitor the performance metric itself, with a threshold calibrated to a known false-alarm rate.

Statistics · 5 min

CUSUM in five minutes

The detector at the heart of my thesis fits in one line, and the idea behind it is very intuitive: small bad months add up.

Read the note

Each month I measure how far the metric fell below its normal level, in standard deviations: y = (μ₀ − x) / σ. The CUSUM keeps a running sum, S = max(0, S + y − k). The allowance k = 0.5 stops normal noise from filling it up, and the max(0, …) means good months can't be "saved up".

When S crosses a threshold h, it raises an alarm and resets. I choose h so that, on the model's own history, a healthy model triggers about one false alarm every 120 months.

A rule that only looks at the latest month needs one extreme value to react. CUSUM reacts to a run of slightly weak months, which is how real decay usually looks. The price is a delay: the smaller the drop, the longer it takes to be sure.

Validation · 4 min

Measuring a detector when the answer is unknown

On real data nobody knows the month a model started to decay, so you can't directly measure how late an alarm was. Here's the workaround I used.

Read the note

I took the real noise of the series, resampled it in six-month blocks to build 1,000 new histories with the same texture, and injected a drop of known size at a known month. Then I counted how long each detector took to fire. This is a standard technique from quality control, applied to a financial series.

Then I checked the calibration out of sample: I set the thresholds on alternating 12-month blocks and measured on the others, and the other way round. The promised one-false-alarm-every-120-months came out anywhere between about half and double.

The lesson I took from it: a false-alarm rate is an estimate, not a guarantee. In practice it needs monitoring too.
